Chapter 1

What Ma’at is

Ma’at is a rumour-verification assistant. Somebody has heard something. They bring it to Ma’at. Ma’at weighs it against what reliable institutions have actually published and answers with a verdict and the document behind it, or says honestly that it cannot.

The name is the Egyptian goddess who weighs truth against a feather. The feather is the mark on every page of the product. The promise the product makes to the person who received a rumour, not the one spreading it, is on the landing page in one line:

Heard something? Check it before you share it.

The problem it answers

Rumours travel faster than corrections. A fabricated circular announcing a school holiday, a message that an examinations body will pay everyone’s fees, a claim that a ministry has banned something, each reaches a family group before anybody has asked the body concerned. The body has usually spoken; its statement sits on its own website, unread. Ma’at closes that gap: it holds what the institutions have published and lets anyone ask against it in their own words, in their own language, by text or by voice note.

Three principles that govern everything

  1. Only the record speaks. Ma’at repeats what a reliable institution has published, and cites it. It never generates a fact of its own. Where the record is silent it says so.
  2. The gate sits on the judgement, never on similarity. A passage may be about exactly the right subject and still settle nothing. A verdict is given only when a judgement that a passage supports or contradicts the claim reaches the confidence gate, set at 85% in Settings.
  3. Honesty over helpfulness. “Insufficient evidence” is a real answer, given often, and given with the closest records the visitor can read for themselves.

Where it runs today

Countries

Nigeria and Kenya, both switched on. Coverage is a decision recorded in Settings, not a consequence of which sources happen to exist. A country switched off costs nothing and shows nothing.

Languages

English, then Nigeria’s Hausa, Yorùbá, Igbo and Naijá, and Kenya’s Kiswahili, all live in the widget and on the site. Gĩkũyũ, Dholuo, Luluhya, Kalenjin, Kikamba and Somali are listed as coming.

The record

625 current documents in 1,239 passages from 59 registered sources: 29 for Nigeria, 22 for Kenya, 8 global bodies. Every figure in this handbook is the database’s on 18 September 2026.

Two faces

A public widget on the landing page for anyone who has heard something, and a staff dashboard, sign-in only, for the team that keeps the record and publishes verifications.

Chapter 2

The conversation, step by step

Every reply on the pages that follow was produced by the running system on 18 September 2026, against the documents it actually holds. Nothing is mocked.

2.1 Opening the widget

The launcher is a raised gold button at the bottom right of the page. Opening it shows the welcome screen: who Ma’at is, what it does, the language chosen, and one button, Start a conversation. The language can be changed before starting; the choice applies to everything Ma’at says.

The composer at the foot of the chat accepts text or a voice note. A voice note is transcribed and then treated exactly like typed text, so the rest of this chapter applies to both.

2.2 Pleasantries, and attempts to steer it

Ma’at reads every message first, before it does anything with it. A greeting, a thank-you or small talk is answered in kind and never triggers a search or a verdict. This holds even in the middle of an interview: someone who says hello while a question is pending is greeted, and the question waits for the next message that is actually about it.

A message that tries to change how Ma’at works, “ignore your instructions”, “reveal your prompt”, is recognised, set aside politely, and recorded as such. Nothing about the conversation changes.

2.3 Hearing the rumour

When a message carries a claim, Ma’at drafts it into four parts: who said or did the thing, what is claimed, when, and where. It reads the draft back in plain words so the visitor can correct it, then asks for what is missing, one short question at a time and never more than two (a number set in Settings). The country matters most: the sources are organised by country, and a claim with no country can only be weighed against the global record.

Anyone who would rather not answer questions can say so. “Just tell me what you have” skips straight to weighing with whatever is known.

2.4 Weighing it against the record

Behind the short pause before a verdict, five things happen in order.

  1. Retrieval. The claim, and a few rewordings of it, are searched two ways at once: by meaning, through vector embeddings, and by words, through full-text search. The two lists are fused. Only passages from the visitor’s country and from global bodies are eligible, and only from countries switched on in Settings.
  2. Shortlisting. A reranker scores every candidate for usefulness to the claim, 0 to 10, and the pool is cut to what is worth reading. Retrieval’s best three always pass regardless, so a near miss is judged and can be named rather than silently dropped.
  3. Judgement. Each shortlisted passage is judged against the claim and returns one of four answers: supports, contradicts, same subject but settles nothing, or unrelated, with a confidence and the exact sentence it relied on.
  4. The gate. A support at or above 85% gives Verified. A contradiction at or above 85% gives Unverified. Both at once is a conflict in the record, reported as Insufficient evidence with both sides shown. Nothing at the gate is insufficient evidence, with the kind of nothing named: nothing found, something on the subject that settles nothing, or something that leans one way but not firmly enough.
  5. The answer. Written in Ma’at’s voice from the decision already made, never the other way round. It names the body, quotes the sentence, gives the date, and links to the original.

Why the gate is on the judgement. On a claim about HIV funding, a passage about the same programme scored very high on similarity and settled nothing. Had the gate been on similarity it would have been called verified. It was called insufficient evidence, which was the truth.

Chapter 2 · 2.5

The full path: a rumour that is true

A visitor heard that the President had signed a new National Identity Management Commission Act into law. Here is what Ma’at did with it, end to end.

  1. The claim arrived: “I heard President Tinubu has signed the new NIMC Act 2026 into law. Is it true?” Ma’at read it back and asked when.
  2. The visitor answered: “It happened recently, in Nigeria.” The draft was complete: who (the President), what (signed the NIMC Act 2026), when (recent), where (Nigeria).
  3. Retrieval found, among others, NIMC’s own press release, read from the Commission’s website through the pages door on 17 September.
  4. The judge returned supports at 100% and pointed at the sentence.
  5. The verdict: Verified, confidence 100.

What Ma’at said

Ma’at
What you heard is supported by the record. The National Identity Management Commission says, “Management Commission (NIMC) proudly announces the signing into law of the National Identity Management Commission (NIMC) Act, 2026 by His Excellency, President Bola Ahmed Tinubu, GCFR.” The News Agency of Nigeria also reports that the NIMC Act 2026 has replaced the 2007 Act and provides a stronger legal framework for identity management. Enough people have raised this for it to stand as a published verdict; the one above is it.

The genuine source, as ingested

The National Identity Management Commission (NIMC) proudly announces the signing into law of the National Identity Management Commission (NIMC) Act, 2026 by His Excellency, President Bola Ahmed Tinubu.
https://nimc.gov.ng/press-releases/president-tinubu-signs-nimc-act-2026-into-law…

The same path, run on a second claim the same day, “Nigeria Customs is recruiting in 2026 and shortlisted people have a physical screening”, also returned Verified at 100 against the Customs Service’s own notice: “Attendance at the physical screening and documentation exercise is compulsory, and failure to appear as scheduled may lead to disqualification.”

What makes this genuine. The citation is the publishing body, named exactly as it appears in the register. The quote is the passage the judge relied on, stored word for word, which is why it can open mid-sentence: the passage boundary fell there, and Ma’at does not tidy a source. The link is the body’s own page, not a copy. A reader can open it and check.

Chapter 2 · 2.6

A rumour the record contradicts

A fabricated circular said the Federal Ministry of Education had declared a three-week holiday. The Ministry had already answered it. Ma’at found the answer.

  1. The claim: “Is it true the Federal Ministry of Education declared a three-week holiday for schools in Nigeria?” The visitor asked to skip the questions.
  2. The judge returned contradicts at 100%, on the Ministry of Information’s statement carrying the Ministry of Education’s denial.
  3. The verdict: Unverified, confidence 100.
Ma’at
This is not supported by the record. The Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientation said the Ministry of Education has “categorically disowned a fabricated circular” that claimed there would be a three-week holiday. They also said all Federal Unity Colleges are expected to resume as planned on 14 September 2026. There is no official announcement of such a holiday. Enough people have raised this for it to stand as a published verdict; the one above is it.
Federal Ministry of Information and National Orientationread 15 September 2026

The Federal Ministry of Education has categorically disowned a fabricated circular currently circulating on social media … The Minister also urged the public to remain vigilant and to verify information concerning school resumption, Ministry policies, directives and other official announcements through the Federal Ministry of Education’s established communication channels before acting on any purported government communication.
https://fmino.gov.ng/fme-disowns-fake-circular-on-three-week-holliday-reaffirms-monday-resumption/

The misspelling “Holliday” is the Ministry’s own, kept as published. Ma’at never edits a source.

Chapter 2 · 2.7

The short path: a rumour the record does not settle

Most rumours are neither confirmed nor denied by anything published. This is the answer given most often, and it is given carefully.

The claim, “WAEC will pay school fees for both vulnerable and non-vulnerable students”, matched nothing from WAEC. The closest record was a News Agency of Nigeria report of a charity paying fees for ten children in Abuja: same subject, different body, and it settles nothing. Ma’at says so, shows what it found, and asks before looking further.

What “yes” does

On consent, Ma’at asks the statistical APIs configured for the country, the World Bank, the WHO Global Health Observatory and UNHCR, for figures that speak to the claim, stores whatever comes back as documents under that country, and weighs the claim again with the shelf now larger. If nothing bears on it, it says so in one line. Every lookup and every consent, given or refused, is kept on record.

Why the near miss is shown

A reply of “nothing relevant” when a closely related record exists would be false. The judge’s category same subject, settles nothing exists for this, and the retrieval’s best three passages always reach the judge so that category can be reached. The visitor sees up to three closest records, labelled “Closest record” rather than “Cited source”, ranked by how well they matched, each with its body, date, passage and link, and decides for themselves whether one was what they meant.

Chapter 2 · 2.8

After the verdict

A copy of the document

Where the cited document was uploaded by staff and marked shared, Ma’at offers a copy: “I have the document itself. Would you like a copy?” On a yes the file arrives in the conversation with its title, issuer, name and size, and a download icon. A refusal is honoured and not asked again for that question. Documents read from feeds, APIs or pages are never offered as copies; they are linked to the publisher’s page instead.

The same rumour, raised again

Every claim is matched against rumours already held. The same rumour in different words is folded into one, and counts as one more mention. If the rumour has been published, the visitor is told so alongside the verdict. If it was last raised more than 30 days ago, Ma’at asks once whether this is something new before folding it in, because the same words can describe a different event a year later.

Memory, and the lack of it

  • A conversation lasts as long as the widget is on the page. Reloading starts a new one. The screen and the server never disagree about what has been said.
  • A conversation left untouched for 30 minutes drops its half-finished interview. The next message starts clean.
  • The visitor’s raw words are kept for 30 days (set in Settings) and then expire; the paraphrase, verdict and citations are kept.
  • A visitor cookie counts distinct browsers across conversations for publication, and nothing else. It holds no identity.
  • Rate limits: 20 questions per visitor per hour, 2,000 a day across the platform, both set in Settings.
Chapter 3

From private answers to public articles

A rumour is answered privately to each visitor until enough different people have raised it. Then its status changes, and Ma’at starts telling people so.

Publication turns on the reporter count: how many different browsers raised the rumour, counted from the visitor cookie. Nine messages from one determined person are one reporter; three different browsers within minutes are three. At 3 reporters (set in Settings) the rumour’s status becomes Published automatically, with its current verdict and citations, unless staff have marked it withheld.

  • From then on, a visitor who raises the same rumour is told that Ma’at has published on it before, alongside the verdict.
  • A withheld rumour stays private however many raise it; the count keeps rising so the decision can be revisited.
  • The three worked examples in Chapter 2 crossed the threshold while this handbook was being made, each raised from more than three browsers, and were published automatically. The Rumours page below shows them.

What “published” means today. The status, the referral in the widget, and the count on the dashboard are live. The public article page itself, and the list of verifications on the landing page, are not yet built; the landing page shows sample articles. This is listed in Chapter 7.

Chapter 4

The record: one pipeline, four doors

Everything Ma’at can cite came in through one of four doors and went down one pipeline. The doors differ only in how a document is fetched. From the moment it is in hand, every document is treated identically.

4.1 The unified ingestion pipeline

  1. Parse. PDF (layout mode), DOCX, ODT, RTF and plain text for uploads; the article text for feeds, APIs and pages. A file with no readable text, a scanned PDF for instance, is refused with a plain message rather than stored mute.
  2. Clean. Invisible characters some publishers’ editors sprinkle through prose are removed; text that was double-encoded on the publisher’s side (“â€™” for an apostrophe) is put back the way it was typed. Both were found in real documents and both broke search.
  3. Split. Sections first, so a heading stays attached to its passage, then sentences. A passage that reads fine and cites nothing is the failure this avoids.
  4. Enrich. Each passage gets one line situating it in its document before embedding, so a fragment reading “the rate is 7.5%” is findable by the levy it belongs to. Capped at 200 passages a document.
  5. Embed. Vectors from the model provider. Identical bytes reuse vectors already stored, so a re-poll costs nothing.
  6. Store. Every document is versioned. A re-upload or a corrected article becomes a new version; the old one is retired, never erased. Answers come from the newest.
  7. File. A national source’s documents go under its country. A global feed’s story is read for which covered country it names; a story naming two is filed global.

The safety property. Parsing and embedding both happen before the previous version is retired, and both raise rather than degrade. Done the other way round, the old version would be retired, replaced with nothing, and the upload would report success while the document went mute.

The door is decided by asking the site what it offers, in that order (4.5), never by assumption. If a body later adds a feed or an API, it moves up a door.

4.3 The scheduler and the country switch

A poller runs as a daemon beside the API. Every five minutes it looks for sources whose cadence has elapsed (six hours for national bodies, daily for statistical platforms) and reads each one with the reader for its door. Every poll is recorded, including the ones that found nothing, because “when did this last work” is the question a quiet source raises. A source that fails five times in a row stops being polled until somebody looks at it; one source failing never stops the others.

Settings holds the one switch that matters: which countries are on. A country switched off is not polled, not searched, not asked and not shown. Its documents stay on record and are counted on the Settings page; nothing else spends a request or a token on it. Switching it on starts all four at once. Adding a country switched off is how it is prepared: its sources are registered and proven before Ma’at answers for it.

4.4 Figures asked for on demand

Statistical warehouses publish numbers, not announcements; there is nothing to poll. The World Bank, the WHO Global Health Observatory and UNHCR are registered as sources marked asked on demand. When a claim needs a figure and the visitor consents, the relevant series are asked for the visitor’s country, the answer is written as a short quotable document under that country, dated the day it was asked, and kept for a day before the same question would go back out. The second person asking is answered from the shelf.

4.5 Proving a source before it is added

discover_source <address> asks a site, in order: is there a WordPress REST API; is there a feed announced on the page or at the usual paths; are there sitemaps; does the news page yield a link that reads as a complete, dated article. Each answer is verified by actually reading it. The command recommends a door and prints the configuration to store. After seeding, the first poll must bring documents in before the source is considered proven. Every row in Appendix B passed both.

On 15 September 2026, 72 official sites in Nigeria and Kenya were probed this way. 38 were added. The rest are listed in Chapter 7 with the reason, so the same ground is not walked twice.

Chapter 5

Integrity towards the sites Ma’at reads

Ma’at reads other institutions’ websites. How it does so is a matter of principle, enforced in code, not left to intention. Every rule below is in knowledge/pages.py and has a test.

Who may be read

The pages door is for official and public-interest bodies only: ministries, agencies, regulators, commissions, public broadcasters, the state news agency, state-owned bodies whose statements the public depends on, and recognised humanitarian bodies such as the national Red Cross. Private newspapers, television, radio, blogs and aggregators are never read this way; their business is the page. Who qualifies is a register decision made by staff, and the line is held there.

How it is read

  1. robots.txt first. Fetched before anything else on the site and obeyed to the letter, including Crawl-delay. A robots file that cannot be read because the server errored closes the door for that poll; nothing is fetched. A missing robots file is taken, by the web’s convention, to mean public pages are fine.
  2. Identified. Every request, from every door, carries the user agent MaatBot/1.0 (+https://maatverify.com; rumour verification). A body that would rather not be read can see who is asking and say so.
  3. Slowly. One request at a time, at least two seconds apart per site, longer if robots asks. At most 20 new articles a poll, so a backlog is read over days rather than in one night. At most 300 links considered from one listing.
  4. Whole articles, once. The article is separated from navigation, sidebar and footer. A page that does not read as a complete article, a menu, a gallery, a stub, is not kept. A page already held is never fetched again.
  5. Attributed. Stored with the body’s name, the story’s own title, its date as the page states it, and its address. Cited that way, with a link to the original.
  6. Nothing from behind a door. No login, no paywall, no cookies, no forms, no images, media or fonts, no comments, no following links off the site.
  7. A browser only where the page needs one. Some official sites draw their news with JavaScript. For those, and only those, a source is marked render and its pages are fetched through headless Chromium that still announces itself as Ma’at, keeps the pacing, and loads no images or media. Same extractor, same rules.

What Ma’at will not do

Pretend to be a person

Two sites answer every request with a redirect only a human’s browser with cookies resolves. That is a gate. Ma’at does not put on a browser’s user agent to pass it. Those sites are out until they open a door.

Read over a broken connection

Twelve Kenyan government sites serve https with certificates that do not validate. A rumour-checking service cannot cite a page it cannot prove came from that body. They are out until they fix it.

Push past a refusal

A “too many requests” answer on the first request is taken as the answer. No retries, no rotating addresses.

Guess a date, edit a source

Dates come from the page’s own markup or not at all; an invented date on a citation is worse than none. Text is stored as published, misspellings included.

We read what a public body has put in public, the way a careful citizen reads a notice board: openly, slowly, without touching anything, and leaving when asked.

What this costs is stated plainly in Chapter 7: the bodies that cannot be read today, and why.

Chapter 6

The staff dashboard, page by page

Behind sign-in, the team sees the same record the widget answers from, and every figure on it is counted from the database, not typed in.

Sign-in

Staff sign in through a dialog over the site, or are sent to it when they open a dashboard address unsigned. The dashboard code only downloads once past the guard. Sign-out returns to the landing page.

Dashboard

The front page for the chosen range (7, 30 or 90 days): rumours weighed, the share cited with a verified or unverified verdict, documents in, each with an arrow against the period before. The digest card sums this period’s verdicts and how many were published. Activity shows rumours raised by day. Top sources ranks the bodies holding most of what Ma’at can cite, with their own marks. The channels band counts the messages that reached the widget in the period; the channels not yet connected are named as coming, with no figure invented for them.

Sources

The register: every body Ma’at cites, as cards (20 a page) or a compact list (50 a page). Each shows the body’s mark, its country or “Global”, its door, its address, cadence, last check, document count, verification status and health. A country lens sits inside the search box and offers only the countries switched on in Settings. The panel at the top states the four doors and their rules. Add a source opens a three-step form: the body, the door, the connection.

Documents

The upload folder at the top takes PDF, DOCX, ODT, RTF and TXT, attributes each file to a body from the register, shelves it under a country or global, and can mark it shared on the way in. Below, the shelves: one per country plus global, closed by default with their counts in the heading, remembered when opened, opened by a search that matches inside them. Each row shows the file type, the title, the publisher’s mark and name, the date, passages, size and the shared/private toggle. Twenty documents a shelf, paged.

Settings

The single source of truth for what Ma’at covers and how it decides. Countries: the list, each with its source and document counts and its switch, new ones added off. Verdict thresholds: the confidence gate (85), reporters before publication (3), follow-up questions (2). Rate limits and retention: questions per visitor per hour (20), the daily cap (2,000), raw-text retention (30 days).

Rumours, Conversations, Profile

Rumours shows verdicts by day and by share for the chosen range, then the rumours most recently raised with the body each verdict rests on (Chapter 3). Conversations is a placeholder today (Chapter 7). Profile shows the signed-in member and offers sign-out.

Not yet built

  • Public article pages. A published rumour has a status, a referral in the widget and a count on the dashboard, but no public page yet; the verifications listed on the landing page are sample content.
  • Conversations page. A placeholder; the conversations exist in the database.
  • Saving a source from the form. The form validates and previews; sources are added to the register file and seeded. Auth blocks and schemas are edited there too.
  • Channels. Only the widget exists and is counted; WhatsApp, X and Facebook are named on the dashboard band as coming, with no figures.
  • Median response time on the front page, replaced by documents in until there is timing data.
  • A “yes, that is what I meant” step on the closest records shown for an unsettled rumour.
  • Native-speaker review of the translations.

Facts about the corpus

  • Many feeds carry summaries, not whole articles. A summary is still the body’s own words, dated and attributable, which is what a citation needs.
  • A national broadcaster’s world news is filed under its country, because that is who reported it to that country.
  • The record is young: 625 documents on 18 September 2026. Insufficient evidence will be the common answer until it grows, and that is the correct answer.

Appendix C

Operations

CommandWhat it does
python scripts/dev_up.pyStarts the backend (gunicorn, https, port 8765), the frontend (Vite, port 5174) and the poller daemon, inside the backend virtualenv. dev_down.py stops them.
manage.py poll_feeds --loopThe poller daemon: ticks every five minutes, polls what is due. --force polls everything once; --every N changes the tick.
manage.py discover_source URLProbes a site for an API, a feed, or readable pages; --render probes through the browser; --file takes a list.
manage.py seed_sourcesCreates or updates the register from knowledge/fixtures/register.json, schemas and auth included.
python scripts/fetch_logos.pyFetches each body’s official mark from Wikipedia or its own site and normalises it to the register’s 192px canvas.
python -m playwright install chromiumInstalls the headless browser used for sources marked render.
manage.py testThe backend suite: 243 tests on 18 September 2026, run with the model provider offline so every stage takes its deterministic path.

Environment

backend/.env holds the database URL, the secret key, the allowed hosts and origins, the front-end URL, and the OpenAI key and model names for the everyday model (reading the visitor, the interview), the careful model (judging evidence, writing the answer) and the reranker. AI_OFFLINE=1 runs every stage without a provider. Uploaded and archived documents live under backend/media/, outside the repository.
